The Idea

In 2019 the people searching "how do I fix my audio in OBS" were landing on forum threads and three-year-old YouTube videos. I'd been streaming for two years and researching audio gear for longer, and I could write. Streamer's Haven started on April 16, 2019 as a place to answer those questions properly: one article per question, researched, tested, illustrated, and written for the person asking it.

I wrote under the pen name Mr. Goodhand. The tagline was "Don't Chase Growth, Chase Improvement," and the site practiced it: no ad spend, no growth hacks, just the next useful article.

The Build

I designed and coded a custom WordPress theme from scratch rather than adapting one. Dark, with a single orange accent. The homepage sends new streamers to a beginner's guidebook, audio articles, and recommended tools through photo cards and a full-width guide banner. The article template is built for long reads: an auto-generated table of contents, slanted orange section headings, styled comparison tables, and a sidebar of latest and related posts. Under that, a member area with registration and profiles, a partnered-communities page, and ad and affiliate placements designed into the layout rather than bolted on.

Owning the theme meant every SEO fix the content side needed, from heading structure to page speed, was a change I could make myself the same afternoon.

The logo is mine too, and I'm still proud of it: a webcam, an eye, and a Wi-Fi signal in one mark, for a streamer broadcasting to the viewers watching them.

The Writing

For three years I was the entire editorial staff: 246 articles, every one researched, written, edited, illustrated, and optimized by me. The content was organized around the questions people actually search:

  • OBS Studio tutorials: audio filters, noise gates, the expander, 60fps settings, the virtual camera, plugin roundups.
  • Stream setup guides: internet speed, Discord servers, Twitch tools, channel points, moderation commands.
  • Hardware buying guides: microphones, headphones, capture cards, lighting, and PC components, with pros and cons per pick.
  • VST plugin walkthroughs, YouTube growth, platform news, and public-service pieces on account security, DMCA, and FTC disclosure rules.
  • A beginner's guidebook tying the categories together for someone starting from zero.

Every piece started with keyword research and competitive analysis in Rank Math, Keywords Everywhere, SEMrush, and Ahrefs. Google Search Console steered the content calendar toward the gaps: what people were searching that nobody had answered well yet.

Monetization

Gear guides and setup tutorials carried Amazon affiliate links, placed where they answered a real buying question rather than sprinkled through the text. Display ads started on Google AdSense; once the site cleared Ezoic's traffic minimum, I moved it to their platform for placement testing and higher revenue per visitor. Both streams were running when the site sold, which is what made it worth selling.

The Sale

In 2021 I packaged the site, its traffic history, its ad and affiliate revenue, and its content library for a buyer, negotiated the deal, and handed it off to its new owner. The one sale where the product was something I'd built myself. Different from closing at a counter, but the same job underneath: know what it's worth, show the proof, and get to yes.
