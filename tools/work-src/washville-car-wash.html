The Job

A tunnel car wash, newly opened, averaging about 600 cars a day. I didn't wash any of them. My job was the booth at the pay station: greet every car, sell the ones that made sense on an unlimited membership, and move the rest up from the $10 base wash to something better. Every car was a conversation of about twenty seconds, and the conversion rate was the scoreboard.

The product was simple: unlimited washes for one vehicle, billed monthly, no contract, cancel any time, in four tiers from Downtown up to Skyline. The launch offer was "20/20/20": Skyline, the top tier, for $20 a month for the first three months, then the regular $39.99. A single Skyline wash was $25, so at either price the membership paid for itself on the second wash. An incentive that sells itself is the whole pitch; my job was to say it clearly.

The Pitch

I didn't sell by being pushy. I sold by being direct, telling people plainly when the membership wasn't for them, and asking questions that had yes-or-no answers. The script I settled on was two qualifying questions and one offer:

MeHello, welcome to Washville! This your first time here?

If yesAwesome. So we're running a promotion right now, but before I get into that, I just want to make sure it's worth your time. Do you live in the area, or are you visiting?

If localOkay, well, in that case the promotion is a good fit. Twenty dollars a month gets you unlimited Skyline washes for your vehicle, every day, for the first three months. After two washes it pays for itself. If you plan to wash your car at least twice a month, it's worth it. If not, a single wash is what I'd recommend. There's no contract, so you can cancel any time. Sound like something you want to do?

Each question does a job. First time here? tells me whether I'm pitching or just processing a regular. Live here or visiting? screens out the people the membership would waste money on, and says out loud that I'm checking. Then the offer states the math, names the case where the answer is no, and asks a question that can only be answered yes or no. People who've been told honestly when not to buy are much easier to close when the answer is yes.

Reading the car

The membership came in tiers, Downtown, Midtown, Uptown, and Skyline at the top, and the wash menu ran the same way from the $10 base wash to the $16–$25 packages. The script above says "the promotion"; which tier I actually named depended on what pulled up. A Lamborghini is getting pitched Skyline at $39.99. A rust bucket is getting Downtown or Midtown, and which one depends on the state of the interior: someone who keeps the inside clean will pay for the outside. For the genuinely nice cars, the Supra that came through, the customers who clearly cared about the paint, I'd recommend the touchless wash: same price, same wash, rollers switched off, nothing touching the clear coat. It costs the wash nothing and it's the reason that customer comes back.

The rule for the upsell itself was features as benefits: not what the package includes, but what the car looks like when it comes out the other end.

In Spanish

Enough of the customers spoke Spanish that I learned the pitch in it, on the floor, from the customers themselves. It's conversational at best, but a stripped-down version of the same script worked: Hola, bienvenido a Washville. Veinte dólares por mes, por tres meses, no contrato, cancelar cuando quiera. Price, term, no contract, cancel any time. It usually worked, because the offer is the same in any language.

Opening Day

The location opened with a free-wash promotion, and part of my day was standing at the road with a FREE WASH sign, shaking it at traffic. It felt ridiculous. It also worked: someone shouted from their car, "For real? Free wash?" and I shouted back, "For real for real, come on in and we'll get your Chevy looking sparkling clean." Every car that pulled in on a free wash was a membership conversation at the booth, and I closed 49 memberships that day.

The rest of the winter was the booth, which had a heat pump, and the same twenty-second conversation a few hundred times a shift.

Results

Over eight months I closed 23.1% of the cars I pitched on a membership, with peak days over 50%. That put me third in the company overall, across its locations in multiple states. The two people ahead of me worked New York sites doing 800+ cars a day against our 600; the gap was volume, not close rate. Selling the same product from a smaller lane and landing third is the number I'd point a hiring manager at.
